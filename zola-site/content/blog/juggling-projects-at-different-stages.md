The simplest project diagram has one person and one project. That is a useful
starting point because it lets the work have a clean shape. There is a current
state, a short-term next state, and a medium-term direction. The person can ask
what needs to happen next, what progress looks like, and whether the challenge
is still close enough to their ability to produce flow.

Real work rarely stays that clean. One person has many projects. Each project is
at a different stage. One is exciting because it is new. One is tiring because
it is almost done but not quite. One is necessary because other people depend on
it. One used to be motivating and now feels like maintenance. Then a team forms
around the work, people join, people leave, projects change owners, and some of
the memory is carried by humans while some of it is carried by artifacts.

That is not just a project management problem. It is a human systems problem.

## Load Is Temporal, Not Just Quantitative

Counting projects is too crude. Five small projects can feel lighter than two
projects if all five are in a stable stage and the next action is clear. Two
projects can feel heavy if one is ambiguous and the other is in a fragile
handoff window.

The missing dimension is time. A project has a past, a current state, and a
possible future. A person also has those same three layers. When one person is
attached to several projects, they are not holding several identical objects.
They are holding several timelines.

That is why motivation changes by project. A new project may provide mastery
because the learning curve is alive. A mature project may provide autonomy
because the owner knows the terrain. A declining project may still provide
purpose if people depend on it, or it may drain energy if its reason has gone
unclear. The emotional load is partly the work itself and partly the stage of
the work.

A useful diagnostic is to ask, for each project, which time frame is causing the
friction. Is the past unclear because nobody remembers why the decisions were
made? Is the current state unclear because ownership is split? Is the future
unclear because the project has no believable next chapter? Those are different
problems, even if they all feel like being busy.

## Groups Carry Memory Unevenly

The next diagram adds people. One person over time becomes a kind of distributed
group: past self, current self, and future self handing context forward. A team
multiplies that pattern. Several people are each carrying their own timelines,
and the project has to survive across all of them.

That is where churn hurts. New people join with no memory of the early choices.
People move teams and take undocumented context with them. Ownership shifts, and
the project becomes a story told by fragments: a ticket, a dashboard, a meeting
memory, a comment thread, a build failure, a document that may or may not still
be true.

The answer is not to make every human remember everything. The answer is to make
context transportable. Clear decisions, useful documentation, visible ownership,
and honest status updates are not overhead in this model. They are how a group
stays coherent across time.

This connects to [Locus of Control at Work](/blog/locus-of-control-at-work/).
One person cannot control every project stage or every team change. They can
control how they leave context behind, how they ask for missing context, and how
they name the part of the work that has become ambiguous.

## The Practical Move Is Stage Awareness

When several projects compete for attention, the instinct is to rank them by
urgency. That matters, but it is not enough. The better first move is to name
the stage each project is in.

Is this a discovery project, where the next action is to learn? Is it a delivery
project, where the next action is to execute? Is it a stabilization project,
where the next action is to reduce risk? Is it a handoff project, where the next
action is to preserve memory? Is it a retirement project, where the honest work
is to close the loop instead of pretending it will restart?

Different stages need different behavior. Discovery needs questions. Delivery
needs commitment. Stabilization needs evidence. Handoff needs narrative.
Retirement needs closure. Treating all of them as generic tasks creates the
feeling of juggling identical balls when the objects are not identical at all.

One person, many projects, many people, and time across all of it. That is the
real shape. The goal is not to make it simple. The goal is to make the temporal
load visible enough that the next action fits the stage of the work.
