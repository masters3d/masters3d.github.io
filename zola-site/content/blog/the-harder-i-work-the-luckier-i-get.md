A project wrapped up recently, and when someone asked how it went so smoothly, I
heard myself say, "Honestly, we just got lucky." Everything had lined up. The
teams we depended on were ready when we needed them. The decisions that usually
stall for weeks landed in days. All the stars aligned.

Then I thought about it for a second longer, and the sentence did not hold up. I
had worked hard on that project, and most of that work was not code. I had
worked on aligning people. I had worked on getting everyone on the same page
before it mattered. I had spent time with the PMs, and with each of the teams
involved, and I had been proactive about the conversations that tend to show up
late. When the moment came and the other side happened to be ready to receive
what we were sending, that was not a coincidence. A lot of it was the result of
work I had already done.

There is an old line for this, usually credited to Thomas Jefferson even though
nobody has found him saying it: "I'm a great believer in luck, and I find the
harder I work, the more I have of it." I think it is true, and I think both
halves are true.

## Luck is real, and it is not the whole story

I do not want to pretend luck does not exist. It does, and it is a big component
of a lot of success. Timing, the people you happen to work with, a reorg that
goes your way, a dependency that ships early: none of those are fully in your
control. Pretending otherwise leads to the opposite mistake, where every success
is proof of your own brilliance and every failure is somebody else's fault.

But "we got lucky" quietly erases the part that was not luck. In engineering, a
lot of what gets called luck is a choice that turned out right: you picked the
solution that held up, or you engineered the path the project took. A dependency
team being ready is often the trace of a conversation you had a month earlier. A
fast decision is often the result of the context you put in front of the
decision maker before they needed it. The stars aligning is, a lot of the time,
people aligning, and someone did that aligning.

The way I think about it now is in terms of odds. Diligence, planning ahead, and
paving the path for a project do not guarantee anything. They change the odds.
Each conversation you have early, each dependency you confirm before it blocks
you, each piece of shared understanding you build ahead of time, takes a way the
project could fail off the table. When enough of those are gone, the remaining
outcomes are mostly good ones, and from the outside (and sometimes from the
inside) that looks exactly like luck.

## Luck compounds when you build on earlier wins

The other half is that the work carries forward. A project that went well leaves
things behind: trust with the teams you worked with, relationships with the PMs,
a pattern that worked, a reputation for following through. The next project
starts on top of all of that. People answer faster because they remember the
last time. Dependencies line up sooner because the path already exists. I wrote
about this kind of carrying forward in
[Leverage and the Stairs You Build](/blog/leverage-and-the-stairs-you-build/):
each step you build makes the next wall easier to climb. Building on top of
earlier success is how you get there faster, and it looks a lot like a lucky
streak to anyone who did not see the stairs being built.

This is also why I think the attribution matters, and why I do not want to leave
"we just got lucky" as the official story. If the success was luck, there is
nothing to repeat. If the success came from aligning people early and working
the dependencies before they were urgent, then that is a practice, and a
practice can be done again, taught, and noticed. The same problem shows up in
how work gets rewarded. As I wrote in
[Quality Engineering vs Engineering Quality](/blog/quality-engineering-vs-engineering-quality/),
the work that prevents problems is mostly invisible when it succeeds, and
crediting the outcome to luck makes it even more invisible.

## Saying it more accurately

I still think there is room to say "we were lucky." There will always be
projects where the timing simply broke our way, and saying so is honest. What I
want to stop doing is reaching for luck by reflex, as if the groundwork never
happened. A more accurate version is something like: "A few things broke our
way, and we were ready for them because of the work the team put in early."

That sentence gives credit to luck where it belongs and to preparation where it
belongs. It also tells the people who did the aligning, the planning, and the
proactive conversations that the work was seen. And for me, it is a reminder of
what to do next time. The harder I work on the parts nobody sees, the luckier
the next project tends to get.
