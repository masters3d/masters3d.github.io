Ten years ago, I was still explaining why I wanted to become a programmer. I had
learned Python and Swift, built iOS apps, contributed to open source, and
finished programs that said I was ready. I could make software, but I was still
trying to understand whether I belonged in software engineering.

The strange part about looking back now as a senior engineer is that code was
never the hardest part of the transition. Code was the visible obstacle. The
deeper work was learning to trust my ability to enter an unfamiliar system,
build a useful mental model, make a decision with incomplete information, and
remain responsible for what happened next.

My [origin story](/blog/from-creative-tools-to-software-engineering/) ends where
this one begins. That story was about discovering that programming had been
hiding inside my creative work all along. This story is about what happened
after software engineering became the work.

## The skills I thought would matter

At the beginning, I measured progress through languages and tools. Python was a
breakthrough because I could understand it. Swift was a breakthrough because it
made systems programming ideas approachable. Git, the command line, web APIs,
frameworks, and data structures each looked like another gate I needed to pass.

Those skills mattered. They gave me a vocabulary and made me useful. They also
gave me a comforting scoreboard: another language learned, another application
shipped, another technical problem solved.

The scoreboard became less useful as the work became more consequential. The
hard problems were no longer contained in one function or one repository. They
crossed teams, release schedules, customer needs, operational constraints, and
years of decisions nobody had written down. Knowing more syntax did not resolve
ambiguity. It did not create shared understanding. It did not decide which
problem deserved to be solved.

## What senior meant in practice

I once imagined seniority as technical certainty. A senior engineer would know
the answer quickly, recognize every pattern, and make difficult work look easy.
Ten years of engineering taught me almost the opposite.

Seniority meant getting comfortable saying what I did not know while still
creating a path forward. It meant separating reversible choices from decisions
that would constrain a system for years. It meant making the work legible so
another person could challenge it, continue it, or operate it after I moved on.
It meant noticing when the actual problem was not code.

The title was not the destination. It was a larger radius of responsibility. My
work was judged less by whether my part compiled and more by whether the whole
system produced the intended result. That included the interfaces between
software and people: plans, reviews, incidents, handoffs, mentoring, and the
small decisions that teach a team what it values.

I also had to unlearn the idea that struggling with a subject proved I lacked
the capacity for it. My freshman-year experience had turned difficulty into a
verdict. Engineering eventually turned difficulty into information. Confusion
meant the model was incomplete. A failure meant the system had taught us
something. Not knowing was a starting condition, not an identity.

## The creative path was not a detour

Before software engineering, I was a video editor. For a long time I treated
that as the career I had before the real technical career began. I do not see it
that way anymore.

Editing taught me to work from an intended experience backward. It taught me
that every cut has a cost, that pacing changes meaning, and that the audience
does not care how complicated the timeline was. Digital media taught me to move
between a creative goal and the constraints of a tool. Church media work taught
me to make something useful for a community rather than for the satisfaction of
the maker.

Those lessons transferred directly. Software is full of technically impressive
work that does not help the person on the other side. The creative background
kept asking a useful question: what are we trying to make possible for someone?

The list of careers I wrote when I was young was not evidence that I lacked
focus. It was evidence that I was looking for a medium broad enough to hold all
of my interests. Software engineering became that medium.

## Ten years did not finish the work

A decade is long enough to recognize patterns and short enough to know how many
remain invisible. I have more judgment than I had in 2017, but judgment has made
me less interested in pretending that any one framework, language, or process is
the answer. Context keeps winning.

The biggest change is that I no longer need programming to prove that I am
technical. I care more about whether the work is worth doing, whether the system
can be understood, whether the team can own it, and whether the people around me
are growing. I still love languages and tools. They are just no longer the
scoreboard.

Ten years ago, I was afraid that the black screen of a terminal meant I did not
belong. Now it is simply one of many places where the work can begin. The lesson
was not that fear disappears after enough time. It was that fear can become
curiosity, curiosity can become practice, and practice can become
responsibility.

---

_The first decade did not turn me into the person who always knows. It turned me
into someone who knows how to keep moving, how to make the path visible, and how
to bring other people along._
