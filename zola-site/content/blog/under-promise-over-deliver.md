In late October 2026 I said no to a ten-minute fix. Some drives kept getting
locked up, and I had been helping unblock them. I wrote a guide so that anyone
could do the unlock, and I did it myself a couple of times so I knew the guide
worked. Then the request came back. Nobody knew how to read the guide, nobody
had the context I had, and could I unlock the drives one more time?

I could have. With the context in my head it was probably ten minutes of work.
There was pressure to just do it, and doing it would have made everyone happy
for the afternoon. I did not. I asked for two days and one more person, and said
that with those we would make this problem go away for good.

That choice is one small case of a rule I have come to hold firmly: do not
over-promise. Under-promise, and over-deliver.

## A promise sets the floor

You never know what is going to happen. You do not know which blockers are
waiting, which dependency will slip, or how much of what you promised you will
actually be able to deliver. A promise made at the top of your range has no room
for any of that.

Say you promise at your maximum and then, working as hard as you can, you barely
deliver it. The signal you send is "I kept my promise." That is all anyone can
see. They do not know it was your maximum, and they do not know how much work it
took to get there. From the outside, what you delivered looks like your normal.
Your maximum has become their minimum.

Two things follow from that, and neither is good. First, there is nothing extra
for anyone to reward. You said you would do it, you did it, and the account is
settled. The effort that went into it is invisible, so it earns nothing. Second,
a baseline invites more. If this is what you do normally, the next request
assumes you can do at least this much again, probably a little more. But you
were already at the top of your range. There is no more. Now you are the person
who delivered last time and cannot deliver this time, and the difference is
something only you can see.

Flip it around. Promise what you can deliver at your regular capacity, with room
for the blockers you cannot predict, and you can always deliver. When things go
well and you finish early or do more than you said, that difference is visible
to everyone, because you set the expectation below it. Over-delivery becomes a
plus: something people can attach to you and reward. The work is the same. The
promise decides whether anyone can see it.

## Plan for your regular week

The capacity you plan against has to be your regular capacity. Account for the
circumstances, the effort, and the unknowns, and then size the promise to a
normal week. Do not plan for 60-hour weeks. A plan that needs a 60-hour week has
already failed; it just has not found out yet. I learned the cost of living on
that kind of plan the hard way, and it was less about the hours than about
[losing control of where my time went](/blog/burnout-is-a-control-problem/).

There is a quieter version of the same trap. If you do the work in silence and
do it a little faster each time, every speedup becomes the new expectation. Next
time they want it faster, and then faster again, with no more people and no time
set aside to automate it. Nobody is being unreasonable. The people asking do not
have the context to know how long the thing takes. You are the only one who
does, which means setting the expectation is on you, not on them.

So when someone asks for something faster, I try to say what is actually true:
we can go faster if we get more people, or more time to prepare in advance. That
is the only way this gets faster. Sometimes I could go faster on my own by
burning my evenings, but saying that out loud would set the wrong expectation,
because it would tell everyone the speed comes free.

There is one boundary on "more people," and it is an old one. Fred Brooks
observed in
[_The Mythical Man-Month_](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL21231757M/The_mythical_man-month)
(1975) that adding people to a late software project makes it later, because the
new people need to be taught and the team needs more communication to stay in
step. That is why the time to ask is when the promise is being made, not after
the date is at risk. More people up front is a resourcing decision. More people
at the end is a rescue, and rescues are slow.

## Solve it once

Back to the drives. If I had spent the ten minutes, I would have become the way
the drives get unlocked. The next lockup would have been an emergency with my
name on it, and the one after that too. I do not want to be an emergency. I have
written about why
[being good in a crisis is not the same as wanting to live in one](/blog/good-at-it-is-not-the-same-as-wanting-it/),
and why a person standing in for a missing fix is
[a system using a human as glue](/blog/minimize-humans-as-glue/).

The guide had been my first attempt at handing off the fix, and it did not work
as a handoff, because the people reading it did not have the context it assumed.
That was useful information. It meant the fix could not be "teach people to do
it by hand." It had to be "make it so nobody does it by hand." So I asked for
the thing that would solve it forever: one more person and 24 to 48 hours. That
was a smaller promise than "I will keep unlocking them whenever you ask," and a
much more honest one. It traded a short, visible delay now for the problem
leaving entirely, which is the trade the
[question, delete, simplify, accelerate, automate](/blog/elons-algorithm-manual-process/)
sequence keeps pointing toward.

What I am really saying is: do not undermine yourself. Always give yourself the
time to solve the problem once, so you do not have to keep solving it. Take the
hit up front, ask for what it actually takes, and promise against your regular
week. People will judge you against what you say you can do. Make sure what you
say leaves room for you to do more.
