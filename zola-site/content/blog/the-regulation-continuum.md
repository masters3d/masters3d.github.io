It is tempting to divide motivation into two boxes: intrinsic is good, extrinsic
is bad. That version is easy to remember and usually wrong.

Self-Determination Theory, from Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, offers a more
useful model: a continuum of regulation. Some motivation is fully external. Some
starts outside us but becomes internalized. Some is so integrated with identity
that it feels like part of who we are. The practical question is not whether
every task is intrinsically enjoyable. The question is where the reason for
doing it lives.

That distinction matters at work because many valuable tasks are not naturally
fun. Writing tests, updating runbooks, reviewing dependency changes, and
documenting decisions may not always feel like play. But they can still become
deeply owned.

## External and Introjected

External regulation is the simplest form: do the thing to get a reward or avoid
punishment. "I write tests because the pull request checklist requires it." The
reason lives outside the person. Remove the enforcement, and the behavior
weakens.

Introjected regulation moves the pressure inward, but not in a healthy way. "I
write tests because I would feel like a bad engineer if I did not." The person
is no longer only avoiding an external consequence. They are protecting an image
of themselves. That can look responsible from the outside, but the fuel is
guilt, anxiety, or ego defense.

These two forms are brittle. They can produce compliance. They can be necessary
for minimum standards. But they do not create the kind of steady ownership that
survives ambiguity.

The warning is not that checklists are bad. Checklists are useful. The warning
is that a checklist is only the beginning of motivation, not the end. If the
reason never moves beyond enforcement, the person will do exactly what the
checklist asks and little more.

## Identified and Integrated

Identified regulation is where the reason becomes personally endorsed. "I write
tests because I want to ship reliable software." The activity may still have
started as an external expectation, but the person now sees the value. The work
connects to a standard they care about.

Integrated regulation goes one step deeper. "I write tests because that is how I
build things." The behavior has become part of identity. It is not necessarily
enjoyable in every moment, but it is coherent with the person's sense of craft.

This is the part the simple intrinsic-versus-extrinsic split misses. Identified
and integrated motivation are not pure intrinsic motivation. The original push
may have come from a team norm, a mentor, a painful incident, or a review
process. But once internalized, the behavior can be just as durable as a
naturally enjoyable activity.

That is good news. It means teams do not have to wait for every important
practice to become fun. They can help people understand the value, exercise
control, and connect the practice to identity.

## Intrinsic Is Not the Only Goal

Intrinsic motivation sits at the end of the continuum: the activity is done
because the activity itself is interesting or enjoyable. Some engineering work
genuinely has that quality. Debugging a tricky race condition can feel like a
puzzle. Designing a clean abstraction can feel satisfying before anyone else
sees it.

But making intrinsic motivation the only acceptable state creates its own
distortion. It can make people feel defective for not enjoying every responsible
behavior. It can also lead managers to ignore the design work required to help
external requirements become owned standards.

The better goal is internalization. Move the reason rightward on the continuum.
External pressure becomes understood value. Understood value becomes identity.
Sometimes identity becomes enjoyment. Sometimes it does not, and the work is
still sustainably motivated because it is yours.

## Moving the Reason Inward

A task moves inward when three conditions are present. The person understands
why it matters. The person has enough autonomy to shape how it is done. The
person can connect the task to a standard, community, or outcome they care
about.

That suggests a different way to introduce expectations. Do not only say, "write
tests because the checklist says so." Explain which failures tests catch, show
where a missing test once let a bug escape, let the engineer choose the test
shape, and recognize the judgment behind the test, not just its existence.

The same applies to documentation. A requirement to update a runbook may begin
externally. It becomes identified when the engineer sees how the next on-call
person depends on it. It becomes integrated when leaving the system
understandable is simply part of the engineer's definition of done.

_The regulation continuum is useful because it removes the false choice between
pressure and passion. Work often starts as an outside expectation. The question
is whether the system leaves it there, or helps the person turn it into a reason
they can honestly call their own._
