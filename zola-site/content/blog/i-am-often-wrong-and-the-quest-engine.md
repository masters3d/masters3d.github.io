Boris Chernyak
[wrote about his approach to every problem](https://borischerny.com/management,/product/2026/09/19/I-am-often-wrong.html):
understand the available information, gather missing information, define the
problem, define a clear and simple approach, define a goal, act with urgency.
When new information surfaces, he goes back and redefines the problem, the
approach, and the goal, then repeats. He calls the resulting churn healthy, as
long as you know it's part of the process. That description is a
[Quest Engine](/blog/quest-engine-introduction/) with the seams still showing.
Six steps, but three moves repeated twice.

## The Six Steps Collapse Into Three Moves

Line the six steps up against Search, Drive, and Renew and they pair off
cleanly.

| Chernyak's Step                    | Quest Engine Move | Why It Fits                                                       |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1. Understand the available information | **Search**    | Take stock of what's already known before touching anything else |
| 2. Gather missing information       | **Search**          | Go find what you don't yet know; close the gap                    |
| 3. Define the problem                | **Drive**           | Convert information into a single, sharp target                    |
| 4. Define a clear and simple approach | **Drive**          | Commit to one path instead of a menu of options                    |
| 5. Define a goal                    | **Drive**           | Set the success criteria before you start moving                   |
| 6. Act with urgency                 | **Drive**           | Execute; the goal is worthless without deployment speed            |
| "Go back and redefine 3-5, repeat"  | **Renew**           | Check outcomes against expectations, then feed the update back in  |

Steps 1 and 2 are Search: understanding what's available and gathering what's
missing are the same move, run twice, once against what you already have and
once against what you don't. Steps 3 through 5 are Drive: problem, approach,
and goal are three facets of a single act of commitment, the thing you point
your urgency at. Step 6 is Drive's execution half, the "act" that turns
commitment into motion. The loop-back ("along the way I will often learn new
information, that means going back and redefining #3-5 and repeating") is
Renew, named exactly for what it is: new data forces you to update your
priors, and the update has to reach the plan or it doesn't count.

## Why the Thrash Is Healthy

Chernyak's key insight is that redoing steps 3 through 5 can feel thrashy, but
that the thrash is healthy if you understand it's part of the process. That
matches how [Renewal](/blog/quest-engine-introduction/) works in the Quest
Engine: renewal isn't a phase that runs once at the end, it's a continuous
check that happens every time the world hands you new information. The
[failure mode](/blog/quest-engine-the-why/) isn't the redefinition itself, it's
mistaking redefinition for a lack of a plan when actually it's the plan working
as designed. A person who never revises 3 through 5 after new evidence isn't
being decisive, they're refusing to renew, and every prior they never update
compounds into a plan that no longer matches reality.

This is also why Chernyak singles out failure to clearly define the problem
(step 3) and failure to define a simple approach (step 4) as the most common
failure modes he sees. Both live inside Drive, and Drive is the move most
visible to other people (it's the plan you present, the goal you commit to).
[Search](/blog/quest-engine-introduction/) failures are often invisible until
someone points out a gap in the plan; Drive failures are the plan itself, so
they're what gets critiqued. When Drive is unclear, complexity creeps in and
success criteria go soft, exactly the symptom Chernyak names: "it leads to
complex plans and unclear success criteria."

## Feedback Is How You Search Your Own Blind Spots

Chernyak notes that clarity is hard to see in your own plan and that this is
why getting feedback from other people matters. That's a second-order Search
move: instead of searching the world for missing information, you're searching
other people for the gaps in your own Drive. He asks for this feedback in
return, and he tries to give it in real time so the team can renew faster.
Real-time feedback shortens the loop between acting and renewing, which is the
whole point of running Search, Drive, and Renew as a tight cycle rather than a
long one. A team that waits until the postmortem to renew has already paid the
full cost of the miscalibration; a team that renews continuously catches the
drift while it's still cheap to correct.

## Meta-Renewal: The Loop Renews Itself

The most Quest Engine-shaped line in the whole piece is the closing one: "If
part of this meta-process is meta-wrong, I am open to changing it." Chernyak
doesn't just apply Search, Drive, Renew to the problem, he applies Renewal to
the framework itself. The loop is willing to update its own definition, not
just the plan running inside it. That's the same property that keeps the
[Quest Engine self-similar at every scale](/blog/quest-engine-introduction/):
a single code review, a product launch, and a six-step personal heuristic all
run the same Search, Drive, Renew pattern, and all of them stay honest only
because Renewal is allowed to reach back and revise the process, not just the
output.

## The One-Line Summary

Understand and gather is Search. Problem, approach, and goal is Drive. Going
back to redefine 3 through 5 when new information arrives is Renew, and the
willingness to revise the whole six-step process is Renew applied to itself.
Loving being wrong isn't a personality quirk, it's what it looks like when
Renewal is working.
