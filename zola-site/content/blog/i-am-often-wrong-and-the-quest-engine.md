Boris Chernyak opens
["I am often wrong"](https://borischerny.com/management,/product/2026/09/19/I-am-often-wrong.html)
with a claim most people would bury, not lead with: he's wrong, often, and he
loves it. Then he lays out the six-step system he runs on every problem he
touches. Understand the available information. Gather what's missing. Define the
problem. Define a clear and simple approach. Define a goal. Act with urgency.
Six labeled steps, delivered like a checklist.

Here's why it's worth stopping on: the checklist isn't the interesting part. The
interesting part is the sentence right after it, the one that almost reads like
an aside. New information shows up, he goes back and redefines steps three
through five, and repeats. He calls the resulting churn healthy. Most people
treat that kind of redo as a sign the plan failed. He treats it as the plan
working. That single reframe is the whole reason this essay is worth reading
twice, and it's also, underneath the six labels, a
[Quest Engine](/blog/quest-engine-introduction/) with the seams still showing.

## What Six Steps Are Actually Doing

Six steps looks like a lot to hold in your head. It isn't, because the six
collapse into three moves, run twice.

Steps 1 and 2, understand and gather, are the same move: **Search**. Take stock
of what you already know, then go close the gap on what you don't. Steps 3
through 5, the problem, the approach, the goal, are three faces of a single act
of commitment: **Drive**. Step 6, act with urgency, is Drive's follow-through,
the part where the plan actually leaves the page. And the line that looked like
an aside, go back and redefine 3 through 5, repeat, is **Renew**: the move that
takes what just happened and feeds it back into the plan.

| Chernyak's Step                         | Quest Engine Move | Why It Fits                                                  |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1. Understand the available information | **Search**        | Take stock before you touch anything else                    |
| 2. Gather missing information           | **Search**        | Go close the gap on what you don't know yet                  |
| 3. Define the problem                   | **Drive**         | Convert information into one sharp target                    |
| 4. Define a clear and simple approach   | **Drive**         | Commit to one path, not a menu of options                    |
| 5. Define a goal                        | **Drive**         | Set the success criteria before you move                     |
| 6. Act with urgency                     | **Drive**         | Execute; a goal nobody deploys against is just a wish        |
| "Go back and redefine 3-5, repeat"      | **Renew**         | Check outcomes against expectations, feed the update back in |

That's the pattern most readers stop at: six steps, three moves, done. But the
Quest Engine is [self-similar](/blog/quest-engine-introduction/), which means
the same shape that describes the whole loop also describes the pieces inside
it. Zoom into steps 3 through 5 and the mirror repeats. Defining the problem is
Drive's own Search (scanning what you gathered for the one target worth
committing to). Defining the approach is Drive's own Drive (the actual
commitment). Defining the goal is Drive's own Renew (the check you'll measure
against later). Three steps that looked like a flat list turn out to have the
whole engine folded inside them.

## Why Renew Deserves the Same Weight as Drive

Read the essay once and Drive looks like the star. Five of the six labeled steps
sit inside or next to it, and "act with urgency" is the line that sounds the
most like leadership advice. Renew gets one sentence near the end. It would be
easy to walk away thinking Drive does the real work and Renew just cleans up
after it.

That reading gets the essay backwards, and here's why it matters. Chernyak
insists on a "clear and simple approach," and a simple plan is only safe to
commit to if you know it will get checked and corrected. Renew is that check.
Take it away and "keep it simple" quietly turns into "hope you got it right the
first time." The two failure modes Chernyak names as most common, a poorly
defined problem and an unclear approach, are both failures that live inside
Drive, but they're only visible once Renew runs and holds the plan up against
new evidence. A bad Drive with no Renew behind it doesn't fail loudly. It just
runs to completion, unnoticed, while everyone stays busy.

Renew also does something Drive never has to: it turns on itself. Chernyak
doesn't just apply the loop to the problem. He closes by applying it to the
loop: "if part of this meta-process is meta-wrong, I am open to changing it."
That's Renew, reflected onto the very method that produced it. Drive is never
asked to redefine what commitment means. Renew gets asked to redefine the whole
system, and does it without flinching.

So when Chernyak says "act with urgency," read the fine print. Urgency only
stays honest if the correction loop underneath it is just as fast. He says he
tries to give feedback in real time so his team can catch drift while it's still
cheap to fix, which is Renew setting the tempo, not Drive. Fast Drive paired
with slow Renew isn't speed. It's drift with good posture.

## The One-Line Summary

Six steps looked like a list. It's a loop: Search is understand and gather,
Drive is problem-approach-goal-act with a full Search-Drive-Renew folded inside
it, and Renew is both the correction on the plan and the correction on the
framework itself, carrying exactly as much weight as Drive. Boris Chernyak says
he loves being wrong. What he's actually describing is what it feels like when
Renew is doing its job.
