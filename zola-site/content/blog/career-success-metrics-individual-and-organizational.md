Career growth is often measured at the wrong resolution. The individual asks,
"Am I growing?" The organization asks, "Are people progressing through the
ladder?" Both questions matter, but neither is enough by itself.

A useful career system needs two scoreboards. One belongs to the individual:
capability, autonomy, purpose, collaboration, and knowledge transfer. The other
belongs to the organization: system quality, team productivity, technical debt,
engineering practice, and the health of the environment that makes growth
possible.

If either scoreboard disappears, the system distorts. Individual ambition can
turn into title chasing. Organizational measurement can turn people into
resource planning units. The two have to stay connected.

## The Individual Scoreboard

The individual side starts with technical growth. Is the engineer expanding
capability, building depth in core domains, and gaining enough breadth to
understand adjacent systems? That growth is not the same as years served. A year
can deepen skill, or it can repeat the same safe pattern.

Mastery is one part of the answer. The engineer should be able to point to what
they understand now that they did not understand before, what kind of feedback
they integrated, and which harder problems they can now approach with less
supervision.

Autonomy is another part. Growth should expand the sphere of independent
technical choices. That does not mean escaping review. It means earning trusted
ownership: making decisions, taking responsibility for quality, identifying
improvement opportunities, and guiding others when the work calls for it.

Purpose matters too, though it is harder to measure cleanly. Does the engineer
understand how their work creates user value, business value, or team value? Do
they know why the work matters beyond completing the assigned item? A career can
advance on skill and authority while quietly losing connection to purpose. That
kind of growth may look good in a packet and feel hollow in a life.

The individual scoreboard also includes collaboration and knowledge transfer. At
higher levels, private excellence is not enough. The work has to improve other
people's ability to work. Mentoring, documentation, design explanation, and
better team habits are not side quests. They are evidence that capability is
moving through the system.

## The Organizational Scoreboard

The organization should not declare success just because people receive
promotions. A promotion process can be active while the engineering system gets
worse. The organizational scoreboard asks whether career growth is producing
healthier systems.

System quality is one measure. Are reliability, performance, maintainability,
and operability improving as people grow? Team productivity is another, but it
has to be read carefully. Faster delivery is not a success if it is purchased
with untracked debt, fragile ownership, or burned trust.

Technical debt belongs on the scoreboard because it reveals whether growth is
local or systemic. An engineer who ships impressive work while leaving every
future team with more confusion has not created the same value as an engineer
who ships the work and leaves the path clearer.

Engineering culture is also a metric, though not the kind that fits neatly on a
dashboard. Are code reviews better? Are decisions easier to understand later? Do
people share knowledge before it becomes a bottleneck? Can newer engineers find
a path to competence? These signals decide whether the organization is
developing people or merely consuming their current abilities.

## Growth Strategies Need Both Sides

The classic growth strategies still hold: build mastery, expand autonomy, and
align with purpose. But each one has an individual and organizational version.

For mastery, the individual practices deliberately and seeks feedback. The
organization supplies problems, mentors, review quality, and enough slack for
learning to happen. For autonomy, the individual earns trust through judgment
and follow-through. The organization makes decision boundaries explicit instead
of creating responsibility without authority. For purpose, the individual
connects work to values and outcomes. The organization explains why the work
matters and avoids reducing every goal to delivery pressure.

This is why career development is an engineering systems topic, not only a
manager topic. The system either converts individual growth into better shared
capacity, or it lets growth remain private and fragile.

The healthiest metric is not "how many people advanced?" It is "what can the
system do now because people advanced?" If the answer includes better judgment,
clearer ownership, stronger mentorship, more reliable systems, and less hidden
knowledge, then the career system is working. If the answer is only new titles,
the scoreboard is too small.
