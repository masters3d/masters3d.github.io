On December 30, 2022, I opened a note to myself with the title "Seeking is the
reward." The whole body was one line: "The act of building > than finish it." I
did not explain it. I did not need to. I had just shipped something, and the
feeling I expected at the end had not shown up.

You probably know the moment. The pull request merges. The post goes live. The
project that ate every spare thought for weeks finally becomes a URL, a commit
hash, or a demo. And within an hour your mind is already somewhere else, looking
for the next problem, as if the thing you just finished belonged to someone
else.

For a long time I read that as ingratitude, or a defect in how I was wired. If
finishing was the point, the finish should feel like arriving. It almost never
did. The best stretch was always somewhere in the middle, when the work was
still pushing back and I had not yet found the shape of it. That note was me
admitting it: the reward was not where I had been told to look for it.

## The brain rewards the hunt

It turns out there is a decent explanation, and it is not a character flaw.

The neuroscientist Jaak Panksepp spent his career mapping the emotional systems
that mammals share, and in _Affective Neuroscience_ (1998) he named one of them
SEEKING, in capital letters on purpose. It is the circuit that drives an animal
to explore, forage, and investigate. When it runs, it does not feel like
satisfaction. It feels like eagerness, curiosity, and anticipation, the
leaning-forward feeling of being on the trail of something.

Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson arrived at a related distinction from a
different direction. Their research separates _wanting_ from _liking_. The
dopamine system, which most of us were taught to think of as the pleasure
chemical, turns out to be mostly about wanting: it makes a goal feel urgent and
worth pursuing. Liking, the actual enjoyment when you get there, runs on
different and much smaller circuitry.

I am not a neuroscientist, and I do not want to pretend two lines of research
settle a question about my own motivation. But together they describe exactly
the note I wrote. The engine that gets me to build is a wanting engine, and it
is most alive while the goal is still ahead of me. The finish line hands the
work over to the liking system, which is real but brief. Then SEEKING does what
it was built to do and starts looking for the next trail. The flat feeling after
shipping is not a failure to appreciate the result. It is the system working as
designed.

Steve Jobs gave the original Macintosh team a version of this. Andy Hertzfeld
remembers that after a 1982 team retreat, Jobs summed it up in a handful of
slogans, and one of them was "The journey is the reward." I wrote about another
of his borrowed lines in
[Stay Hungry. Stay Foolish.](/blog/stay-hungry-stay-foolish/), and the two fit
together. Hunger is the seeking. Foolishness is the willingness to start a new
search without knowing where it ends.

## What the search leaves behind

If the reward lives in the search, what exactly does the search produce? A
finished artifact compresses the path that made it. A reader sees the post, not
the four openings I deleted. A user sees the feature, not the designs that
collapsed under their own weight. A teammate sees the migration, not the private
hour when one missing concept finally clicked.

That hidden path is where the builder changes. It is where you learn which
constraints are real and which ones you imagined. It is where the simple version
you dismissed on Monday turns out to be the right one by Friday. It is where
taste gets calibrated, one wrong guess at a time. None of that ships with the
artifact. It ships with you.

This is the part that makes building different from merely producing. Mastery,
one of the three needs I wrote about in
[Find Your Why](/blog/find-your-why-intrinsic-motivation/), is not a pile of
completed items. It is the memory of searches you survived: the ability to
recognize a familiar knot in an unfamiliar problem because you have untied
enough knots before. The output is evidence of that accumulation, not a
substitute for it.

The [Quest Engine](/blog/quest-engine-introduction/), the model I use for how
work moves, has three moves: Search (understand before acting), Drive (act on
what you know), and Renew (make the next cycle better). Seeking shows up in all
three. Search is seeking in its plainest form. Drive is seeking with your hands,
because the world only starts answering once you build something it can push
back against. A broken test, a confused reader, a prototype nobody wants to use
are all answers you cannot get from a plan. And Renew is where the search pays
out: a sharper question, a cleaner instinct, a better next attempt. If the
reward existed only at the finish, Renew would be paperwork. It is not
paperwork. It is the moment the work gives something back.

## Finish anyway

There is a trap in all of this, and I have walked into it. If the search is the
fun part, it is tempting to keep searching forever. Exploration is safe in a way
that finishing is not, because a finished thing can be judged. A wanting engine
with no finish line will happily run on new ideas indefinitely, and a drawer
full of half-built projects can feel like progress while it is happening.

Jobs seems to have known this. On the same short list as "The journey is the
reward" was "It's not done until it ships." The two slogans pull against each
other on purpose, and I think both are needed. "Seeking is the reward" does not
excuse endless seeking. It makes finishing more important, not less. A finished
thing teaches differently than a private draft. It leaves your head and meets a
reader, a user, a teammate, or a future you who forgot why it was built. That
contact is the strongest feedback the search can earn, and you only get it by
shipping. Finishing is how the next search starts with better information than
the last one.

Looking back at that five-word note, I would sharpen it a little. The act of
building is greater than finishing it, but only because finishing is part of the
act. Every good project leaves two things behind. One you can see: the code, the
post, the design. The other you carry: a slightly better engine for the next
search. When the restlessness shows up after you ship something, it might be
worth asking which of the two it is pointing at, and what you would want to seek
next.
