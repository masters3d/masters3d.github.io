_Draft. These are ideas I have not tried yet. This stays unpublished until at
least one of them has a few weeks of practice behind it, and then the post
should report what happened instead of what I hoped would happen._

A few years ago I could not stand at my desk for ten minutes, and I could not
think while standing at all. The discomfort took the attention the work needed.
I wrote about how that changed in
[Quest Engine: Running the Health Quest](/blog/monitor-the-body-running-the-work/):
an adjustable desk, a chair, then a tall stool to lean on, and roughly six
months before I stopped needing the stool and took it out of the room. Standing
has been my default ever since. I still sit in a meeting when everyone else
sits, but at my own desk I stand all day, and it is comfortable.

The part I did not expect is that standing became a sensor. When my eating
drifts and I put on weight, I feel it standing before I see it anywhere else. I
get tired earlier in the day, the way you notice a backpack that is heavier than
it should be. Sitting hides that. A chair carries the extra weight for me, so
nothing tells me to stop. Standing gives me a hard stop at a high level, and I
think it has kept me further along in my own health than I would be otherwise.

That raises a wacky question. If six months of gradual exposure turned "cannot
stand" into "stand all day", what else could I train the same way, during the
workday, without finding extra hours for it?

## Going horizontal: a plank desk

I have worked on my upper body before. I have worked on my legs, and I have done
enough cardio to learn what cardio actually is. The one area I have never
trained on purpose is my core. Not in high school, and not when I lost weight as
an adult. I never got anywhere near a six-pack, and I never wanted to do the
exercises that might have gotten me there.

Then I read about DonnaJean Wilde, a grandmother from Alberta who holds the
[Guinness World Record for the longest abdominal plank by a woman](<https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/longest-time-in-an-abdominal-plank-position-(female)>),
4 hours, 30 minutes, and 11 seconds in March 2024. She
[started planking after breaking her wrist](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/planking-guinness-world-record-donnajean-wilde-grandmother-breaks-record-canada/)
about twelve years earlier, when she could not run or lift, and built up from
there until it was part of her day. In October 2026, at 61, she
[held a plank for five hours](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/nation/2026/10/08/planking-world-record-donnajean-wilde/92154179007/)
in a new attempt that was still waiting on Guinness to confirm it.

That arc looks a lot like my standing story. One minute becomes two, two becomes
three, and eventually the number stops being the interesting part. So the
exaggerated version of the idea is a desk I can work at while planking: go from
vertical to fully horizontal, on my forearms, with the screen and keyboard where
my eyes and hands land. The work would be the timer. Core strength is the one
thing I have never had, and a stronger core should help my back too.

I do not know what that setup looks like yet. Questions for when I try it:

- Where does the screen go so my neck stays neutral? A laptop on the floor, a
  monitor arm pointing down, or a tablet on a low stand?
- Can I type on my forearms, or is this a reading and reviewing posture, with
  standing reserved for writing?
- What is the first unit? Probably one minute every hour, the way the stool gave
  me permission to stop standing without leaving the keyboard.
- What feedback says stop? Lower-back pain or sagging hips should end the set,
  not be pushed through.

## Coming down: the resting squat

The second idea is about flexibility and full range of motion. In many places
outside the West, people rest in a deep squat instead of on a chair, and they
keep that range their whole lives. A study of the Hadza, hunter-gatherers in
Tanzania, found they are
[inactive for close to ten hours a day](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1911868117),
about as much as people in industrialized countries, but they spend much of that
time squatting or kneeling. Those postures keep the leg muscles working more
than sitting in a chair does. The authors suggest that how we rest may matter as
much as how long.

I also read about a runner in their nineties finishing a marathon. I do not
think that is talent alone. The body keeps what you keep asking of it, and gives
back what you stop asking for.

So the two ideas fit together. When the plank gets too hard, I do not go back to
a chair. I come down into a squat. Standing, then plank, then squat, then
standing again. Of the three, that rotation is easiest to imagine working from
home first, where nobody has to understand why the desk is on the floor.

## What would make this a real post

The larger question is the ergonomics of working every day while keeping, or
gaining back, strength and flexibility. Standing taught me that a workday is
long enough to train something if I start small and let the stool go only when I
no longer need it. Planking and squatting are the exaggerated next steps, and I
may find they are too much. Before trying them, I would ask a clinician or a
physical therapist about my back and knees.

_None of this has happened yet, which is why it is a draft. If I set up the
plank desk and keep at it for a few weeks, this post becomes a record of what
the first minutes felt like and what changed. If I never do, it stays here as an
idea I once found interesting, which is fine too._
