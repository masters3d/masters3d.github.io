More than ten years ago (closer to fifteen), I lost 20% of my body weight in
four months. I have tried to repeat that result three times since. Each time I
lost less, and each time it took longer. Twenty percent in four months became
fifteen percent in about nine, then ten percent in about twelve.

I am not going to give the actual weights. They do not matter much to anyone but
me, and they hide the comparison I care about. A percentage tells you how much
of the starting system changed. The effort tells you what it cost. Put those two
side by side across fifteen years and the pattern is hard to miss: the older I
get, the harder the same result is to reach.

A big disclaimer first: I am not a doctor, and none of this is medical advice.
This is one person's record of what he tried, what happened, and what he would
do differently. Your body, your history, and your doctor know things this post
cannot.

## Four Months of High-Intensity Cardio

The first attempt had no secret. For four months I worked out about an hour a
day, five to six days a week. Almost all of it was high-intensity interval
training (HIIT), the kind of cardio where you go as hard as you can for a short
burst, recover briefly, and go again. I followed a Beachbody program. I am not
recommending them; it is simply what I used, and any program built around
high-intensity work would have done the same job.

Food changed alongside the workouts. I stopped eating junk, switched away from
milk, ate a little less overall, and ate more protein. That was the whole plan:
an hour a day of hard cardio and a cleaner, slightly smaller diet.

The part I underestimated at the time was the team. Between five and ten of us
did the program together, consistently. On a morning when I did not want to
start, someone else had already started. The results showed up within the first
four weeks, which kept everyone going, and by the end of four months I had lost
20% of my body weight.

The pro was obvious: I lost the weight, and I lost it fast. The cons took longer
to see. A lot of muscle went with the fat. I was small, and I was not very
strong. And when the program ended, the habit ended with it. I stopped doing six
hours a week of cardio, and over the following years the weight came back.

That last part is the lesson I keep relearning. Getting fit is not something you
do once and then keep for the rest of your life. Whatever produced the result
has to keep running, or the result slowly unwinds.

## Weights Instead of Cardio

Years later, when I decided I needed to lose weight again, I wanted to try a
different way. I did not want to go back to HIIT. This time the only goal was
weights.

I worked out three or four times a week for about 45 minutes, alternating upper
body and lower body days. I used an app called Fitbod on my phone to follow the
program. It tracked which muscles I had trained the day before, which ones were
likely still sore, and which had recovered, and it built the next workout around
that. For equipment I had a Bowflex adjustable dumbbell set, the kind that sold
out during the pandemic home-gym rush. (The company itself
[filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in 2024](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240304359573/en/BowFlex-Inc.-Files-for-Voluntary-Chapter-11-Protection-and-Reaches-Stalking-Horse-Agreement-to-Facilitate-Sale)
and sold its assets.) I also took protein and creatine while I was lifting
heavy.

This attempt took roughly nine months, about twice as long as the first, and I
lost 15% of my body weight instead of 20%. But what I got for that 15% was
different. I gained definition and real strength, mostly in my upper body. I
went from barely managing one pull-up to eight with good form. That felt better
than any number on a scale.

I credit part of that result to the muscle itself. My working theory was that
more muscle burned more of what I ate, so the investment per week was lower and
the return lasted longer. When I eventually slipped, the muscle stayed around
longer than the cardio fitness had the first time.

Then the pandemic hit. I stopped, and from then on the routine never came back
the way it had been. The experiment worked; the schedule did not survive the
disruption.

## Intermittent Fasting and a Clearer Head

Every attempt starts again from zero. The starting weights are not the same each
time, but the moment of deciding "I need to do this again" always comes at the
same place: a point where I no longer feel good about my size.

The third time, I changed only one variable: when I ate. No program, no
workouts, just intermittent fasting. I would wake up, drink water, and not eat
until about two or three in the afternoon. Then I had two meals: a late first
meal that counted as breakfast, and a second one very late in the day. I also
ate healthier, with more protein and more vegetables.

This was the slowest of the three. It took about twelve months to see the
result, and the result was 10% of my body weight. For a few weeks I pushed
further to one meal a day (dinner only), but that did not last. I went back to
two meals, then snacking crept in, and there was a rebound.

I cannot tell you how much healthier I was during that year. I lost weight, but
I am sure I also lost muscle, because I was not training and was trying to keep
my body in ketosis. I was hungry much more of the day. Some of what I read at
the time claimed that long periods of ketosis might extend lifespan. I found
that interesting enough to try, but I have no way of knowing whether it did
anything, and I would not repeat the claim as fact.

The real gain was in my head. My focus became sharp in a way I did not expect.
It was also during this period that I quit coffee, and the fasting routine is
what made quitting possible. (The coffee and sleep loop I was breaking is part
of what I wrote about in
[running the health quest](/blog/monitor-the-body-running-the-work/).)
Eventually the weight came back, as it always had, but the focus and the break
from coffee were worth the year.

## Protein, Creatine, and Fiber

What I am doing now is a variation of the fasting approach, except that I am not
really fasting. Breakfast is a protein shake with creatine, every day. I used to
take creatine only when I was lifting heavy; now it is daily. The
[International Society of Sports Nutrition's 2017 position stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615996/)
describes creatine as one of the most researched supplements and found no
compelling evidence of harm in healthy people, which is the bar I wanted before
taking it every day.

Protein from shakes runs about 50 to 75 grams a day. When I tried to push toward
100 grams, my stomach made it clear it did not like that, so I stay in the range
my body tolerates and let the rest come from food. After the shake there is one
more real meal of the day. Usually it is very rich in protein, but not always.
There is more flexibility than in the strict fasting year, and that flexibility
is part of why it holds.

The change I did not expect to matter was fiber. When I was skipping breakfast,
the meals I did eat had to carry all of my protein, and they ended up very
protein heavy with little or no fiber. That caused enough problems that I would
not recommend the pattern. I will spare you the details. Now I take a zero-sugar
fiber supplement every day, alongside zero-sugar protein, and the small shake
breakfast finally works.

So far, this has been going well. I do not have a final percentage yet, and I am
not going to guess one.

_Looking back across fifteen years, the method changed every time: cardio,
weights, fasting, and now protein, creatine, and fiber. The pattern underneath
did not. Each attempt reset to zero, each took longer, and each moved a smaller
share of my body weight. I cannot fully separate age from method, since the
first attempt was also the most intense, but I also cannot pretend the same
effort buys the same result it did fifteen years ago. What I learned is that a
sprint can start the change, and only a routine I can keep running for years can
hold it. That is the difference
[between knowing the path and walking it](/blog/knowing-walking-returning/), and
why
[consistency only helps when it is pointed somewhere](/blog/determined-driven-consistent/).
You have to walk this one yourself. Once you are on it, you can find the side
paths that let you go long and strong instead of sprinting the first stretch and
stalling the rest. This is my path so far. Everybody has their own, and I hope
you find yours._
