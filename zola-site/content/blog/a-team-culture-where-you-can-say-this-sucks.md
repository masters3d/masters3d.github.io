I want to describe a team culture I really like, because I have been on teams
that had it and teams that did not, and the difference was hard to miss.

The short version: on a good team, you can say "this sucks." Not as a complaint,
and not as negativity. As a signal.

## "This sucks" is a metric

Some examples of what I mean:

- "On-call right now sucks."
- "We have been working too many hours. This sucks."
- "There is a lot of work piling up, and most of it is toil."
- A 👎 on a status thread, or a 🔥 on a message about the backlog.

None of these are attacks, and none of them are giving up. They are
measurements. Each one says that some part of how the team is working has moved
past a threshold, and that someone noticed. A thumbs down is cheap to send and
cheap to read, which is exactly what you want from an early warning.

On a team where this is normal, the signal shows up while the problem is still
small. On a team where it is not, the same information still exists. It just
stays inside people's heads until it comes out as burnout or a resignation
letter. I have written about what happens when
[toil passes half the job](/blog/good-at-it-is-not-the-same-as-wanting-it/);
being able to say "this sucks" is how a team finds out it is getting close to
that line before it crosses it.

## Dedicated time to talk it through

The signal is only half of it. The other half is a place for it to go.

There should be dedicated time where the team can talk through things,
especially when things are not going great. Retrospectives are the obvious place
(and I think they belong in
[the same loop as planning](/blog/sprintspective-one-loop/)), but the format
matters less than the commitment: time that is protected, that is for the team,
and that does not disappear the moment things get busy.

Some decisions are going to be made above the team. That does not change. But
even when the team cannot change the decision, walking through it together feels
a lot better than receiving it alone. A lot of healing happens when a team talks
through something hard or difficult, instead of only focusing on the positive
things. Sorting out what the team
[controls, what it can influence, and what it has to accept](/blog/locus-of-control-at-work/)
is much easier to do out loud and together than silently, one person at a time.

## Celebrate the good, name the bad

This is a balance, not a license to complain. A team needs to celebrate the
things that go well. It also needs to be able to highlight the things that go
badly, in a way that brings people together instead of pulling them apart.

The failure on one side is a team that only talks about wins. When everything
said out loud is positive, the person who feels the problem starts to wonder if
they are the only one. "How come you are the only one feeling this way?" is a
question that makes people go quiet, and it is often not even true. They are
just the first one to say it.

What I want instead is the moment when someone says "this sucks" and someone
else says "actually, you are right, this sucks." Then everyone else feels more
comfortable saying it too, and the conversation can turn to the useful part:
what can we do about it? What can we change about this specific thing, this
emerging issue that just happened?

That turn is the whole point. "This sucks" is not where the conversation ends.
It is how the team agrees on what the conversation is about.

## No culture is also a culture

I have also been on teams with basically no team culture. The work still gets
done. Directions are handed down from above, and there is very little
flexibility to change anything. It is fine, in the sense that nothing is on
fire.

But no culture is a culture. Somebody decided to take that stance, even if
nobody said it out loud.

One version of it I have seen often: the tech lead or the manager decides the
timeline, decides when something will be done, and then the team has to figure
out how to get there. I have not seen that work very well. When the people doing
the work have little say in how much effort something takes or when it is
supposed to be done, they also have little reason to raise a flag when the date
stops being realistic. The signal that would have saved the date is exactly the
one that culture has trained everyone not to send.

## Being who you are on the team

All of that to say: team culture is important, and this is an example of a team
culture I really like. Being able to open up. Being able to express yourself.
Being able to be who you are, and to really gel with the rest of the team,
especially when there is a lot of friction or things are going badly.

A team that can say "this sucks" and then ask "what do we do about it?" is a
team that can get through hard stretches together. I would take that over a team
that is always fine, right up until it is not.
