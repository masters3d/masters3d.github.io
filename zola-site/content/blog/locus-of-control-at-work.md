I used to hear "locus of control" as a personality judgment. Some people have an
internal locus. Some people have an external one. The internal people take
ownership. The external people blame the world. That framing is too clean, and
it is not how work actually feels. Even the psychologist who introduced the
term, Julian Rotter, framed it in his
[1966 monograph](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5340840/) as an expectancy:
whether you expect outcomes to follow from your own actions or from luck,
chance, and other people.

At work, control moves around. You may control the code you write but not the
launch date. You may influence the design but not the staffing. You may accept
the reorg, the budget, the market, the legacy system, or the fact that the
person who knew the old decision left three years ago. None of that makes you
powerless. It just means the first job is sorting the field accurately.

The useful model is not internal versus external. It is control, influence, and
accept, with one extra motion: widening what you control over time.

## Control is smaller than ownership

Control is the part where your action directly changes the result. You can write
the test. You can read the logs. You can make the proposal concrete. You can ask
the next question. You can document the decision while it is still fresh. These
are small actions, but they matter because they do not require permission from
the whole system.

Ownership is larger and more ambiguous. A team may own a service without
controlling the upstream dependency that keeps breaking it. A manager may own an
outcome without controlling the market that shaped it. An engineer may own a
migration without controlling every team that has to adopt it. If we confuse
ownership with control, we punish people for variables they could only
influence.

That is why the sorting step matters. When something is inside control, act.
When it is outside control but inside influence, communicate, demonstrate, and
create conditions. When it is outside both, accept it as a constraint and design
around it. Acceptance is not giving up. It is refusing to spend Drive on a wall
that only Search can route around.

## Influence is where most work lives

The middle circle is the hard one. Influence is slower than control and less
satisfying than a direct fix. It asks for trust, timing, examples, and repeated
translation. You cannot force another team to adopt your design. You can make
the path easier, show the cost of the current path, remove uncertainty, and
leave behind artifacts that make the decision safer.

This is also where feedback loops keep you honest. If the proposal keeps
bouncing off the same audience, the answer is not always "push harder." It may
be that the evidence is wrong, the story is unclear, the cost is landing on the
wrong team, or the organization has a constraint you have not understood yet.
Influence is not weaker control. It is a different kind of work.

That distinction keeps this post separate from
[burnout as a control problem](/blog/burnout-is-a-control-problem/). Burnout can
happen when control over your life collapses and the rest of the system consumes
the slack around you. Locus of control at work is the daily operating model
before that failure. It is how you decide where action belongs while you still
have the ability to choose.

## Widening the circle

The best part of the model is that the circles are not fixed. Skill moves things
inward. A tool can turn a weekly manual process into a command. Context can turn
vague anxiety into a known dependency. Reputation can move a decision from
influence toward control because people trust your judgment sooner.
Documentation can move future work inward because the next person starts with
more than you had.

That is the constructive version of an internal locus of control. Not
"everything is my fault" and not "I can manifest the outcome." It is the habit
of asking what could become controllable if I learned more, built a lever,
earned trust, or changed the shape of the system.

The danger is overreach. Some things really do belong in accept. Timing,
budgets, other people's choices, organizational memory, and market pressure are
not made controllable by wanting them badly enough. A healthy locus of control
includes the humility to stop treating every constraint as a personal failure.

So the practice is simple, even if the execution is not: sort the situation, act
where action is real, influence where relationship and evidence can work, accept
the constraint where neither applies, and then look for the one capability that
would move a small piece inward next time. That is enough. Work becomes less
mysterious when you stop asking whether you are in control and start asking
where control actually lives.
