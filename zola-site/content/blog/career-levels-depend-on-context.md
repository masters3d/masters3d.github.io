A level guide is tempting because it looks like a map. Level 2 means this, Level
4 means that, Level 7 means something larger. The problem is that the map
changes shape when the organization changes. A level is not only a measure of an
engineer's capability. It is a description of what the company is prepared to
ask from that capability.

That is why I do not trust level comparisons without context. The same title can
mean different work at a startup, an enterprise company, a product team, a
platform group, an innovation culture, or a stability culture. The title travels
more easily than the responsibility does.

[Individual Contributor Levels as Changing Scope](/blog/individual-contributor-levels-as-changing-scope/)
framed levels as widening responsibility: task, component, system, systems of
systems. This post adds the uncomfortable qualifier. The scope is real, but the
organization decides what kind of scope is available.

## Startup Scope Is Wide and Thin

In a startup, responsibility can expand faster than the formal level system can
name it. One engineer may touch product code, operations, customer support,
infrastructure, data, hiring, and planning in the same month. That can
accelerate growth because the feedback loop is short and the work is close to
the whole business.

It can also blur evidence. A person may appear senior because they hold many
threads at once, even if none of the systems have reached the complexity that an
enterprise level guide assumes. The breadth is real. The depth may still be
forming. A startup can give someone broad ownership before it can give them a
stable platform, a mature review process, or enough peers to calibrate against.

That does not make the work lesser. It means the level signal has a different
shape. Startup seniority often means making reasonable tradeoffs with incomplete
support. The useful evidence is not only what the engineer built, but how they
created leverage under resource constraints, how much chaos they converted into
repeatable practice, and which future maintenance costs they avoided naming as
speed.

## Enterprise Scope Is Narrower Until It Is Larger

Enterprise career systems usually have clearer level requirements. There may be
calibration meetings, promotion packets, ladders, domain expectations, and
formal manager guidance. That structure can be frustrating, but it also gives
the level language more shared meaning.

The tradeoff is that early and mid-level work may be narrower. An engineer can
spend years inside one domain, one service family, or one part of a large
product surface. That does not mean the work is small. A narrow slice of a large
system can contain more history, risk, compliance, customer expectation, and
operational constraint than an entire early startup product.

Enterprise scope often shows up as durability. Can the engineer change a system
without breaking the surrounding agreements? Can they align with adjacent teams,
respect governance, and improve a mature process without pretending they are the
first person to see its flaws? At higher levels, the scope becomes very large,
but it is not usually the visible hero scope of owning everything. It is the
slower work of making many teams move with less friction.

## Product and Platform Reward Different Signals

Product engineering and platform engineering can also make the same level look
different. Product work stays close to users, revenue, launch timing, and the
question of whether a feature solves the right problem. The feedback may be
visible through adoption, support, retention, sales pressure, or customer pain.
The engineer's context includes product judgment.

Platform work creates value by enabling other builders. The user may be another
engineer. The work may succeed by reducing toil, standardizing a hard path, or
making a class of mistakes less likely. The evidence is often indirect, and that
can make level calibration harder. A strong platform engineer may look less
connected to customer-facing work while quietly increasing the organization's
capacity to ship safely.

The level is not one universal job. A senior product engineer may be strongest
at customer-shaped tradeoffs and rapid iteration. A senior platform engineer may
be strongest at internal contracts, reliability, migration paths, and developer
experience. Both can be operating at the same level, but the work asks for
different judgment.

## Culture Sets the Exchange Rate

Culture decides which evidence is easiest to see. Innovation-focused cultures
reward experimentation, novel approaches, and fast feedback. Stability-focused
cultures reward predictability, careful change, and risk management.
Collaborative cultures reward knowledge sharing, facilitation, and team success.
None of those values is automatically better. Each one changes the exchange rate
between work done and level recognized.

A useful career framework has to adapt without becoming meaningless. The stable
part is expanded capability: deeper mastery, wider autonomy, broader purpose,
better judgment, more useful influence. The variable part is the local terrain.
What counts as a hard problem here? What kind of risk matters most? Who must
trust the decision after it is made? Which work creates lasting value in this
particular system?

That is the question I would rather ask than "what level is this person?" Ask
what context the level is trying to serve. A level is most useful when it tells
a team where responsibility should live. It is least useful when it pretends the
same label means the same job everywhere.
