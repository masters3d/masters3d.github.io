Growing up, two warnings came out of adults' mouths almost as often as "eat your
food":

> _"Te va a llevar el río."_ (The river will take you.)
>
> _"Te va a llevar el tren."_ (The train will take you.)

Neither one was really about a river or a train. They were said the way a parent
says "keep doing that and see what happens." The shape is always the same: there
is something out there that is certain to happen, it does not care about you,
and if you do not do what you are supposed to do, it will happen to you. I still
like these phrases more than any English equivalent I know, because they put the
consequence on something that is already moving.

## The Train Is on a Schedule

Start with the train, because it translates most easily. Trains run on a
schedule. You know when the train is supposed to come, and in many US cities the
crossing tells you again right before it does: bells, lights, gates coming down,
the horn. Nobody is hiding the train from you.

The train also cannot do much about you. Operation Lifesaver, the US rail safety
group, points out that
[a freight train at 55 mph needs a mile or more to stop](https://oli.org/site/ga/ga-ol-statistics)
(about 18 football fields). It cannot swerve. Once it is coming, the only party
that can change the outcome is whoever is standing on the tracks.

That is why the internet loves videos of trains meeting whatever got left on the
crossing: a stalled truck, an empty bus, a car that tried to beat the gate. The
joke always lands the same way. Everyone watching knew how it would end, except,
apparently, whoever left it there.

Time is the train I think about most. It is the most predictable thing there is:
the deadline is on the calendar, the birthday comes every year, the review cycle
comes around, the kids grow up on schedule. The signals are everywhere. If you
ignore them and stay on the tracks, time runs you over anyway (not because it is
cruel, but because it was never going to stop). The train will take you.

## The River Is Always Moving

The river is a different warning. The train comes at a known time; the river
never stops.

Lewis Carroll gave the idea to the Red Queen. The line is usually remembered as
_Alice in Wonderland_, but it is from the sequel,
[_Through the Looking-Glass_ (1871), chapter 2](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/12):
"Now, here, you see, it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same
place. If you want to get somewhere else, you must run at least twice as fast as
that!"

A river works the same way. If you are in the water and you want to stay where
you are, you have to swim upstream. Stop swimming and you do not stay put; you
drift. Spanish has a refrán for exactly this,
[_camarón que se duerme se lo lleva la corriente_](https://cvc.cervantes.es/lengua/refranero/ficha.aspx?Lng=0&Par=58319):
the shrimp that falls asleep gets carried off by the current.

Most of what I have built works like that. A skill I stop practicing gets rusty.
Fitness I stop maintaining fades. A codebase nobody tends falls behind as its
dependencies move. A friendship nobody invests in drifts too. A lot of daily
work is not about making progress; it is about not losing the progress already
made. That is the quiet part of being
[consistent](/blog/determined-driven-consistent/): the current is the default,
and staying in place takes effort.

And the river does not hold steady. Some seasons it picks up speed. A storm
upstream turns it into a torrent: higher, wider, faster. Then swimming at the
usual pace is not enough. You have to swim harder, get out of the water, or do
something else entirely. The river will take you.

## Build for the High Water

Growing up, a lot of people lived near the river, and that taught the other half
of the lesson. If you live by a river, you do not build your house for the river
you see most of the year. For eleven months out of twelve the water is low, and
a house at ground level would look fine. People built on stilts anyway, wooden
piles that lifted the floor well above the ground, because the house has to
survive the twelfth month.

Hydrologists have a name for that mark. The USGS describes the
[100-year flood](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/100-year-flood)
as a flood with a 1 percent chance of happening in any given year, not one that
politely waits a century. You build for the highest the river will reach, not
the level it usually sits at. Otherwise the river takes the house, and
everything you put inside it.

The
[parable of the wise and foolish builders](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+7%3A24-27&version=NIV)
makes the same point with rock and sand: the rain and the floods come for both
houses, and the difference is where and how each one was built. Where you choose
to build, and how, decides whether the flood is an inconvenience or the end of
the house.

So when I say it now, I say the long version: the river will take you, unless
you prepare for it not to. Even on a calm day with no storm, the river keeps
moving, and if you are in it you have to keep swimming upstream. And if you are
standing on the tracks, all the whistles and signals in the world will not stop
the train. Move out of the way, or the train will take you.
