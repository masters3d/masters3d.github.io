In December 2021 I kept a reading list about remote and asynchronous work. The
Microsoft Research material in that list argued that remote work needs more
intentional health habits, clearer meeting design, and better understanding of
side channels such as chat during video calls. Microsoft's parallel chat paper
was especially useful because it treated meeting chat as both promise and peril:
it can include quieter voices, preserve questions, and share links, but it can
also split attention or create a second conversation that changes power in the
meeting. The Twist and Doist writing made the complementary argument from an
async-first company perspective: write context clearly, organize conversations
into durable threads, separate urgency from visibility, and do not make people
prove commitment by being always online.

That is what the moment felt like. Remote work had become normal faster than our
habits had adapted. Everyone had tools. Fewer teams had a theory of how context
was supposed to move.

## Distance Turns Context Into a System

In an office, a lot of context travels through weak channels. People overhear
something. They see who is at a whiteboard. They ask a question while walking
back from coffee. They notice that a decision has emotional weight because of
the room's reaction. Those channels are uneven and exclusionary, but they are
real. When work becomes distributed, the channels disappear or become explicit.

That is why remote work cannot be reduced to video calls plus chat. Video calls
replace some synchronous conversation. Chat replaces some hallway interruption.
Neither automatically replaces the background awareness that lets people
understand what is happening and why.

A distributed team has to decide which context should be pushed, which should be
pulled, and which should be captured as durable memory. Status can be pushed in
a short written update. Deep design context may need a document people can read
and comment on asynchronously. Conflict may need a live conversation, followed
by a written decision. A meeting may be the right tool, but only if the meeting
has a job that cannot be done better as an artifact.

The tool choice is secondary. The context path is primary.

## Async Is Not Silence

Asynchronous work is sometimes described as if the goal were to avoid talking to
people. That misses the point. Async work is not silence. It is communication
with better persistence and more respect for time zones, focus, caregiving,
commutes, energy, and the simple fact that not every useful thought appears on a
meeting schedule.

A good async system makes it easier to know what happened without being present
when it happened. It gives people a place to ask questions that improve the
artifact for the next reader. It separates urgency from visibility. It lets a
decision be reviewed by someone who was asleep, in another meeting, or doing the
kind of deep work that would have been broken by a live interruption.

But async has its own failure modes. Written context can be too long, too
hidden, or too stale. A team can bury decisions in chat threads that are
technically searchable and practically lost. People can use "async" as a reason
to avoid a hard conversation that needed tone, trust, and immediate repair.
Parallel chat in meetings can create a second room where some participants
coordinate while others present to the official room.

Remote work does not remove social dynamics. It changes where they show up.

## Meetings Need a Reason to Exist

The most useful remote meeting is intentional. It exists because a group needs
shared attention at the same time: to resolve ambiguity, build trust, make a
tradeoff, read weak signals, or react to something that is changing too quickly
for an async thread. If the meeting is only distributing information, it is
competing with a document. If the meeting is only collecting status, it is
competing with a written update. If the meeting is only creating the appearance
of alignment, it is probably hiding the absence of a decision.

A remote meeting also needs an afterlife. What changed because people met? What
was decided? What remains open? Who owns the next action? If the only record is
that everyone remembers the conversation differently, the meeting created motion
without organizational memory.

This is where remote work connects back to contextual awareness. Context should
be available in an asynchronous medium in a way that is easy for the consumer
and easy for the contributor to update. Subject matter experts should be
reachable asynchronously for clarification. Synchronous time should be arranged
when the need arises, not used as the default container for every kind of work.

That is not an argument against meetings. It is an argument for making meetings
pay rent.

## The Real Question Is How Context Survives

The 2021 reading list was about remote work, but the durable lesson is broader.
Any team with multiple time zones, multiple disciplines, production load,
parenting schedules, deep work, or people joining after the decision was made
has the same problem. How does context survive distance in space and distance in
time?

A colocated team can ignore that question longer because the office supplies
some accidental context. A remote team feels the failure faster. That is why
remote work is a forcing function. It reveals whether the team knows how to
create shared understanding without relying on everyone being in the same room
at the same moment.

Tools help. Research helps. Meeting advice helps. But the center is simpler:
write down what matters, choose live conversation when live conversation is the
right tool, and make the result visible to the people who were not there. Remote
work succeeds when context is treated as infrastructure, not exhaust.
