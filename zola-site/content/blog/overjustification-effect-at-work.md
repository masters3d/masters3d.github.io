There is a particular kind of reward that makes a good thing worse. Not because
rewards are evil, and not because people should work for free, but because some
work already has a reason inside it. Add the wrong external reason on top, and
the original reason can get quieter.

Self-Determination Theory, from Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, draws the useful
line between intrinsic motivation and motivation controlled from the outside.
The overjustification effect, associated with work by Lepper, Greene, and
Nisbett, names the failure mode: when a reward is added to an activity someone
already finds interesting, the person can start interpreting the activity as
something done for the reward.

At work, that matters because many of the best engineering behaviors start as
internal commitments. Someone likes understanding hard systems. Someone takes
pride in clean tests. Someone enjoys mentoring because explaining a concept
sharpens their own thinking. Those are fragile goods.

## When the Reward Rewrites the Story

The dangerous shift is not from unpaid to paid work. Professional work deserves
compensation. The shift is from "I do this because it matters to the craft" to
"I do this because this is the rewarded move."

Imagine an engineer who genuinely enjoys improving reliability. They read
incidents closely, add tests for failure modes, and tighten observability
because the puzzle is interesting and the users benefit. Then the organization
creates a bonus for reliability work, measured by a simple count of reliability
tickets closed. The behavior may increase at first. But the meaning of the
behavior has changed. The engineer is now invited to ask which work counts for
the reward, which ticket can be closed fastest, and whether unrewarded
reliability work is still worth doing.

The activity did not become bad. The measurement changed the reason attached to
it.

This is why blanket incentive design is risky around work people already care
about. A reward can be useful for work that nobody would otherwise prioritize.
It can focus attention on neglected maintenance. It can signal that the
organization values invisible labor. But when the reward becomes the explanation
for doing the work, it may crowd out the older explanation.

## Control Is the Hidden Variable

The overjustification effect is not only about money. It is about control. A
reward that feels informational can support motivation: "this work was valuable,
and we noticed." A reward that feels controlling can weaken motivation: "do this
to get the prize, or else you are losing."

That distinction explains why recognition can help in one setting and distort
behavior in another. Public praise for a thoughtful postmortem may reinforce the
value of learning. A leaderboard ranking engineers by postmortem count may
convert learning into performance theater. The difference is whether the person
experiences the signal as acknowledgement or as pressure.

This is also why punishment-shaped rewards are so corrosive. If the reward is
framed as the thing that proves commitment, not receiving it can feel like
accusation. The person is no longer choosing the work. They are defending an
image of themselves as the kind of person who does the work.

## Protecting Work That Already Has a Why

The practical move is not to remove extrinsic rewards. Workplaces run on pay,
promotion, titles, budgets, and priorities. The move is to match the incentive
to the motivational state of the work.

If the work is already intrinsically motivating, be careful. Pay fairly. Remove
blockers. Give time, tools, and trust. Recognize the work without turning every
behavior into a contest. Preserve autonomy by letting the person own the method,
not just chase the prize.

If the work is necessary but not naturally interesting, external structure can
help. A checklist, rotation, explicit priority, or reward can make neglected
work visible. Even then, the better long-term move is internalization: help
people see why the work matters, how it connects to their standards, and where
they have real control.

The mistake is using the same incentive for both cases. The work that already
has a why needs protection. The work that lacks a why needs explanation,
ownership, and sometimes structure.

## The Small Diagnostic

A useful question is: after the reward is introduced, what story will the person
tell about why they did the work?

If the story becomes clearer, the reward may be supporting motivation. "This
matters, and now the organization is making room for it." If the story becomes
narrower, the reward may be crowding motivation out. "This matters because it
counts."

That difference is subtle, but people feel it. The same task can move from craft
to compliance without changing its shape. The work still gets done, but the
internal engine that made it sustainable loses air.

_The overjustification effect is a reminder to treat motivation as something
already alive, not as an empty slot waiting for a bonus. Before adding a reward,
ask what reason is already there, and whether the new reason will support it or
replace it._
