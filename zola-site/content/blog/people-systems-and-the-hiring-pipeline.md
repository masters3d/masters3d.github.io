In 2021 notes on career progression, I put a Francois Chollet quote near the
front: technical systems are mostly made of people. The people who design the
product, write the code, maintain the code, and use the product are the real
nodes. The technical artifacts are transient edges. I liked that framing because
it explains why career systems, hiring systems, and promotion systems belong in
engineering conversations. They are not outside the technical system. They shape
who gets to build it.

Another short set of notes from the same period was about hiring. It listed
several pipelines into software engineering: STEM university programs,
internships, software development bootcamps, apprenticeship programs such as
Apprenti, and self-taught paths. The note underneath was the part worth keeping:
different pipelines favor different kinds of evidence. Big companies often have
strong college pipelines. Nontraditional candidates are asked for experience
before they are allowed to get experience.

That is a people system design problem.

## A Pipeline Is a Filter

Hiring pipelines are often described as if they merely discover talent. They do
more than that. They define which signals count as talent.

A college pipeline makes certain signals easy to read: coursework, internships,
CS fundamentals, professor recommendations, career fairs, and interview formats
based on algorithms and computer science concepts. There is nothing wrong with
that path. It produces many excellent engineers. It also creates a default shape
for what "entry level" looks like.

Bootcamp, apprenticeship, and self-taught paths produce different evidence. The
candidate may have a portfolio, a career change story, production experience in
a non-software domain, customer empathy, operations discipline, or unusual
resourcefulness. The evidence can be strong, but it is less standardized. A
company that only knows how to evaluate the college signal will call the other
signals risky.

That risk is sometimes real. It is also sometimes a mirror. The organization may
not have an onboarding system strong enough to hire for potential. It may not
have mentors with capacity. It may not know how to evaluate work samples. It may
be using the interview process to compensate for the absence of a development
system after the person joins.

A pipeline exposes what the organization is prepared to support.

## Entry Level Is Not One Thing

The phrase entry level hides several different cases. A computer science
graduate entering a first job, a bootcamp graduate changing fields, an
apprentice learning inside a structured program, and a self-taught developer
with years of adjacent technical work may all be considered junior. They do not
need the same support. They do not bring the same strengths. They should not be
evaluated as if one path is the original and the others are imperfect copies.

For a new graduate, the system may need to teach production reality: ambiguity,
large codebases, tradeoffs, collaboration, and the difference between a correct
assignment and a maintained service. For a career changer, the system may need
to fill CS fundamentals while preserving the maturity, communication habits, and
user empathy that came from previous work. For an apprentice, the system needs a
clear learning contract. For a self-taught engineer, the system may need to
separate gaps in vocabulary from gaps in ability.

This matters because the hiring process often confuses familiarity with merit. A
candidate who has practiced the expected interview format looks lower risk. A
candidate with different evidence requires more judgment. If the organization
has not designed for that judgment, the pipeline narrows by default.

## Hiring Connects to Career Progression

Hiring does not end at the offer. If technical systems are people systems, then
the hiring pipeline has to connect to onboarding, mentorship, performance
expectations, promotion, and team capacity. Otherwise the company is not hiring
a person. It is buying a lottery ticket and hoping the team can absorb the
variance.

A college internship pipeline works partly because it is a system, not just a
source. There are seasons, relationships with schools, intern projects, return
offers, and expectations for beginner support. Apprenticeships also make the
learning structure explicit. Bootcamp and self-taught pipelines can work well
too, but they need equally explicit support: clear first projects, feedback
loops, mentors, skill maps, and managers who understand what evidence they are
looking for after hire.

The mistake is trying to diversify the source of candidates without changing the
rest of the system. A company cannot say it values nontraditional paths and then
measure every candidate only against the shape of a traditional path. It cannot
hire for potential and then punish the first six months of learning as if it had
hired for immediate replacement capacity.

People systems have to be coherent.

## Who Gets Seen

Those hiring notes were short, but they pointed at a larger engineering
question: who gets seen by the system? A pipeline is not neutral. It has intake
points, filters, interview formats, risk models, and support assumptions. Those
choices determine which candidates become visible and which candidates are
treated as exceptions.

This is not an argument to lower standards. It is an argument to name the
standard more precisely. What does the role need now? Which skills must be
present on day one? Which skills can be learned with the support the team can
actually provide? Which signals predict success in this environment, and which
signals are only familiar because they match the interviewer's path?

When hiring is treated as a people system, the question changes from "where do
we find talent?" to "what evidence can we read, and what growth can we support?"
That second question is harder. It is also more honest. The pipeline a company
builds does not just fill roles. It defines the future shape of the engineering
organization.
