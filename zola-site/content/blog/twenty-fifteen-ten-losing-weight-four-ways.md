_A disclaimer before anything else: I am not a doctor, a dietitian, or a
trainer. This is my story, not advice. Bodies differ, and what worked or failed
for me says very little about what will work for you. Talk to someone qualified
before you change how you eat or train._

Close to fifteen years ago, I lost 20% of my body weight in four months. I am
not going to share the actual numbers. They do not matter much, and how small I
got is not the point. Percentages and effort are the point, because I have done
this four times now, each with a different method, and the percentages tell a
story I did not expect: every time I started over, the same effort moved me
less.

Twenty percent the first time. Fifteen the second. Ten the third. The fourth is
still underway. Part of that is the method, and part of it is simply getting
older. Each attempt also reset to zero. I gained the weight back between rounds,
so every percentage is measured from a new starting point, not from where the
last round ended.

## Round One: An Hour a Day, With a Team

The first time had no secret. I worked out for about an hour a day, five to six
days a week, for four months. Almost all of it was high-intensity interval
training (HIIT) cardio. I used a Beachbody program. I mention the name only
because it is what I used, not as an endorsement; any program that pushed high
intensity would have done the same job.

I also changed what I ate. I stopped eating junk, switched away from milk, ate a
little less overall, and ate more protein. That was it. No magic formula.

The part I underrated at the time was the team. Five to ten of us did the
workouts together, consistently, and that group carried me through the mornings
I would have skipped alone. The results showed within the first month, and by
the end of four months I had lost 20% of my body weight.

The tradeoffs were real. On the plus side, I lost the weight and I was fast. On
the minus side, a lot of muscle went with the fat. I was small, and I was not
very strong. Then I stopped doing six hours a week, and the weight came back.
That was the first lesson: fitness is not something you do once and keep for the
rest of your life. If the routine stops, the results stop with it.

## Round Two: Weights, an App, and a Pandemic

Years later, I decided to do it again, but differently. I did not want to go
back to HIIT. My only goal this time was weights.

I trained three to four times a week for about 45 minutes, alternating upper
body and lower body. I followed a program in Fitbod, an app on my phone that
planned each session, tracked which muscles I had worked the day before, and
estimated which ones were still sore and which had recovered. I used a set of
Bowflex adjustable dumbbells, the kind that sold out once the pandemic started
and everyone tried to build a gym at home. I added creatine on upper-body days
and protein on heavy lifting days.

It was a much better experience in one way: I gained definition and strength. I
went from basically one pull-up to five, which felt like a lot, and eventually
to eight with good form. My upper-body strength was the best part of that round.

The scale moved more slowly. This round took about nine months, roughly twice as
long as the first, and I lost about 15% of my body weight. My working theory was
that building muscle changed the trade: a lower weekly investment with a longer
return, where the muscle I gained would help keep the weight off and come back
faster after a break. Then the pandemic hit, the routine stopped, and the weight
came back anyway.

## Rounds Three and Four: Changing When I Eat

The third time, I changed the variable again. No program, no weights. Only
intermittent fasting.

I would wake up, drink water, and not eat until about two or three in the
afternoon. Then I ate two meals: a late first meal and dinner. I tried to eat
healthier inside that window, with more protein and more vegetables. For a few
weeks I pushed further, to one meal a day (dinner), but that only lasted a few
weeks before I went back to two, and the snacking came back with it. There was a
rebound.

Part of my motivation was what I had read about ketosis. The idea that regularly
flipping the body from burning glucose to burning ketones might be good for more
than weight is real research territory. A
[2019 review by Rafael de Cabo and Mark Mattson in the New England Journal of Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31881139/)
describes that metabolic switch and the cellular responses it triggers, with
much of the strongest evidence on aging and lifespan coming from animal studies.
I found it interesting enough to try. I cannot tell you whether it made me
healthier in that sense.

What I can tell you is the result: about 10% of my body weight, over roughly
twelve months. That was the longest round and the smallest number. I was hungry
much more of the day. I am also fairly sure I lost a lot of muscle, because I
was prioritizing the fast and not training. That matches what one controlled
study found: in the
[TREAT randomized trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32986097/) (Lowe and
colleagues, 2020), people eating within an eight-hour window lost only modest
weight, no more than the comparison group, and lost more lean mass in their arms
and legs.

The best part of this round was not on the scale. It was my head. My focus was
sharp, and it was during this stretch that I quit coffee. I wrote about that
loop of
[poor sleep, coffee, and worse sleep](/blog/monitor-the-body-running-the-work/)
separately. Fasting was what finally gave me room to break it. As a lifestyle
change, it taught me more than it took off.

Every round reset to zero, and I eventually reached the point again where I did
not feel good at my size. So now I am on round four, and it borrows from round
three without the fasting.

I still eat breakfast, but it is a protein shake with creatine, every day. I
take creatine daily now instead of only on lifting days. Between shakes I get
about 50 to 75 grams of protein a day; when I tried closer to 100 grams from
shakes, my stomach did not like it, so the rest comes from food. Then I have one
more real meal, usually rich in protein, with some flexibility on the days when
it is not. It is a variation of intermittent fasting, except I am not really
fasting. I also avoid sugar, including in the protein.

The change that made the rest work is fiber. I take it every day. Fasting
quietly broke this for me last time: when I skipped breakfast, my few meals
became heavy in protein and light in fiber, and that caused problems I will not
go into. I would not suggest repeating that. With a shake, fiber, and one solid
meal, the small meals finally hold together. So far this round has been going
well. I do not have its percentage yet, and I am in no hurry to.

## The Path, Not the Sprint

Looking at the four rounds together, the numbers fell (20%, 15%, 10%) while the
time stretched (four months, nine months, twelve). Getting older is part of
that. But what each round actually tested was a different method, and what each
one taught me was more useful than its percentage:

- **HIIT with a team** moved the scale fastest and took muscle with it.
- **Weights** built the most strength and the most confidence.
- **Fasting** changed my relationship with food and coffee and cost me muscle.
- **Protein, creatine, and fiber** is the first approach I built to last rather
  than to finish.

The weight came back every time the routine stopped, which is the clearest
result in the whole record. The method mattered less than whether I could keep
doing it.

Morpheus told Neo there is a difference between knowing the path and walking the
path, and I have written about
[what comes after walking it](/blog/knowing-walking-returning/). I knew the path
before round one. I knew it better before round four. Knowing never kept the
weight off; only walking did. You have to walk the path to find the side paths
that fit you, and for me the one worth taking now is long and strong, not a
sprint at the start and a stall after it.

_This is one person's record across fifteen years, and everyone's path is their
own. Mine started with a team and a timer, went through dumbbells, an empty
morning, and a cup of coffee I no longer drink, and has landed, for now, on
something I expect to still be doing next year. If you are starting your own
round, the question I would ask is not which method loses the most. It is which
one you will still be walking when the results stop being new. I hope you find
yours._
