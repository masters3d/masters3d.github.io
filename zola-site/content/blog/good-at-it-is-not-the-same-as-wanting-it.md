I can tell you, without much false modesty, that I am a good DRI. DRI stands for
directly responsible individual: the one name attached to a problem when
something has to get done and there is no time to argue about whose job it is. I
do well in emergencies. When the system is down and everyone is looking for the
person who will hold the thread, I like being that person. There is a real
excitement in it. Part of it is the chance to be the hero, and I would be lying
if I said that part does not feel good.

I also do well with date-driven development, especially when the date is
reasonable. Give me a fixed launch and a scope that can actually fit, and I will
get there. I narrow down, I cut what does not matter, and I move.

For a long time I treated those two facts as a description of what I should be
doing. If I am good at it, the reasoning went, then this is my lane. It took me
a while to separate the two halves of that sentence. Being good at something
tells you what you can do. It does not tell you what you want to keep doing.

## What the good part costs

The reason I am fast in an emergency is the same reason I cannot stay in one. My
best guess at what is going on is plain stress physiology: the deadline or the
outage raises my cortisol (the hormone the body releases under stress), and I
drop into solve mode. Solve mode is narrow on purpose. It ignores everything
that is not the current problem, it does not ask whether the problem should
exist, and it does not stop until the thing is fixed. That is exactly what an
incident needs. It is also not a state a body is built to hold for months.

The image I use for it is a little crude. In solve mode I will shovel excrement
really fast to get the path clear to a good spot. I am good at the shoveling. I
can be the fastest shoveler on the team. But being fast at it does not mean I
want to do it for the long term, and the speed itself is a warning sign, because
it comes from the same stress that makes the work feel urgent. I have written
before about how
[burnout was a control problem](/blog/burnout-is-a-control-problem/) for me
rather than an hours problem. Living in emergency mode is one way to lose that
control without noticing: the work picks the schedule, the adrenaline makes it
feel like a choice, and it is only later that you see how much of the year the
crisis decided for you.

So the skill is real, and so is the cost. Both are true at once. Competence is a
fact about what you can do. Preference is a fact about what you can sustain, and
it is worth asking about separately.

## Nobody gets promoted for shoveling

There is a second reason to be honest about this, and it is less about the body
and more about where the work leads. Nobody is getting promoted for shoveling
excrement, no matter how fast they shovel. The thanks are real in the moment,
and the incident review might name you. But the next time the same pile shows
up, the same person gets handed the shovel, because that person is good at it.
Being the best at a manual task is a reliable way to be assigned that manual
task forever.

The people who move up are the ones who mechanize the process. They look at the
shoveling and ask why there is a pile, why a person is doing the moving, and
what machine would make the next emergency smaller or make it never happen. That
is a different kind of work, and it does not come with the same rush. It is
slower, it happens on a quiet afternoon instead of at two in the morning, and it
does not feel heroic at all. It is also the work that compounds.

I made a version of this argument about teams in
[Minimize Humans as Glue](/blog/minimize-humans-as-glue/): a situation that
needs a hero is a situation where a person is standing in for a fix that was
never made. That post was about the system. This one is about the person inside
it, and about why the person can be the last one to want the fix. If being the
hero is the part of the job you are good at and the part that feels best, then
the fix that removes the need for a hero also removes your best moment. That is
an uncomfortable trade, and I think it is the reason a lot of strong responders
keep responding instead of building. The process for building the machine itself
(question the step, delete it, simplify, speed it up, and only then automate) is
the subject of
[Elon's Algorithm](/blog/elons-algorithm-manual-process/); the hard part, for
me, was wanting to start it at all.

## Choosing where the strength goes

I do not think the answer is to stop being good in a crisis. Emergencies happen,
deadlines are sometimes real, and a team is lucky to have someone who can hold
the thread when they do. I still want to be that person when it counts.

The change is in what I let the strength mean. I treat emergency competence as
a tool I am glad to have, not as a job description. When I notice that I am
enjoying the shoveling, I take that as a signal to ask how often this pile shows
up and who is building the thing that moves it. When a date-driven project ends,
I try to spend the next stretch on work that makes the next date less dramatic,
instead of looking for the next deadline to feel useful against.

The question I ask myself now is simple. Not "am I good at this?", because the
answer to that is often yes. The better question is "would I choose this if I
were not good at it?" If the honest answer is no, then the skill is something I
can lend to the team when it needs it, and the rest of my time belongs to the
work of making sure it needs it less.
