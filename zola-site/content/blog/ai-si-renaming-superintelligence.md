On September 29, 2026 an executive order told executive branch departments and
agencies to say "Super Intelligence" instead of "Artificial Intelligence" in
their documents and communications. The day after, California signed its own
order telling state agencies to keep saying "Artificial Intelligence." SpaceX
said it would rename its AI unit to SpaceXSI, and Musk's summary was "No more
AI. SI is better." So the federal government and the largest state in the
country now officially disagree about what to call the same technology.

My high level take: it will not make any difference. SI, AI, ASI, AGI, these are
all made up labels for a moving target. Today the label of the moment is
superintelligence. Tomorrow there will be another level and another label for
it. The technology does not change because the letters changed.

## Why I Think the Rename Is Happening

I do not know the motivation, and I am guessing here. The guess that fits best
is that this is an attempt to shift a mentality. "AI" has picked up a lot of
negative weight. It is the thing that takes jobs, the thing that generates slop,
the thing people are told to be worried about. "Super intelligence" sounds like
an upgrade rather than a threat. If you believe the problem is vibes rather than
policy, renaming is a cheap intervention.

The counterargument is the one California made, which is that renaming is what
you do instead of doing something. I am not going to litigate that here. I am
more interested in whether the new name is actually better.

## AI Is a Term of Art, and Those Are Hard to Move

AI is a term of art. The whole point of a term of art is that everybody uses the
same lingo to refer to the same thing, and the value of it is almost entirely in
the fact that it is shared. It has been in use since the 1960s. Decades of
papers, courses, job titles, org charts, product names, laws, and conference
proceedings are built on those two letters. You do not get to replace that by
decree, and you especially do not get to replace it by decree in one
jurisdiction while the neighboring jurisdiction decrees the opposite.

That said, I do agree with one half of the complaint. AI is extremely general
right now. In practice it means "something the computer does by itself," which
covers a thermostat schedule, a recommendation ranker, a spam filter, and an
agent that opens pull requests. When a word covers that much range it stops
carrying information, and every conversation starts with ten minutes of figuring
out which AI anybody is talking about.

So there is a real problem. I just do not think "call all of it SI" solves it,
because SI is also one label for the same enormous range. You traded a vague
word for a vaguer and more flattering one.

## Name the Levels, Not the Field

The useful distinction is not between AI and SI. It is between the levels, and
those already have names that mostly work:

- **Narrow AI.** Good at one bounded task. Almost everything shipping today.
- **AGI, artificial general intelligence.** Competent across domains at roughly
  human level, including domains it was not specifically built for.
- **ASI, artificial superintelligence.** Beyond the best humans across
  essentially everything.

If I were naming these from scratch I would probably not have used the word
"artificial" as the stem at all. "General AI" and "Super AI," or just "general
intelligence" and "super intelligence" as levels, would have been clearer than
smuggling the level into a three letter acronym and hoping people know which one
you mean. Notice that the new federal term collapses this: it takes the label
for the top level and applies it to the whole field, including the spam filter.
That is the opposite of the precision that motivated the complaint.

The levels are also going to keep multiplying. Whatever gets built after ASI
will need a name, and that name will be made up too. The lesson is that the
field level label is the least interesting one. If you want to communicate, say
what the system can actually do.

## The One Good Argument for SI Is Linguistic

Here is the place where I think SI genuinely wins, and it has nothing to do with
vibes.

"Super" is close to a universal morpheme. It is the same word, or nearly the
same, across the Latin-derived languages and well beyond them. "Intelligence" is
also Latin-rooted and survives translation with its shape intact. So "super
intelligence" transposes unusually well. Not to every language, but to a lot of
them.

Compare that with "artificial intelligence." In Spanish it is _inteligencia
artificial_, so the acronym is IA, not AI. The adjective goes after the noun,
and the initials flip. Every Spanish language document, every bilingual
conversation, every search query has to carry both forms. But "super" always
goes in front, so in Spanish it is _superinteligencia_, and the acronym stays
SI. The word order problem disappears because the modifier is a prefix rather
than a trailing adjective.

That is a small, real, practical advantage. It is probably not why the order was
signed, but it is the argument I would make if I had to defend the change.

## The Association I Cannot Unhear

"Super" also carries baggage, and not the serious kind. The first thing it
reminded me of is Dragon Ball Super, where "Super" is literally the name of the
season that comes after the original run. The second is "supers," the way
superhuman beings get referred to collectively in comics.

The comic book association might be closer to accurate than I would like. We are
heading into a period where people with these tools can do things that look
superhuman relative to what the same person could do five years ago. Not because
the person changed, but because the leverage did. If the word pulls in that
direction, toward augmented humans rather than replacement machines, that is at
least a more honest picture of what is happening right now than either
"artificial" or "superintelligence" suggests on its own.

## Where I Land

AI is a bad name that is too established to replace. SI is a slightly better
name on linguistic grounds and a slightly worse one on precision grounds,
because it hands the top level label to the entire field. Neither one changes
what the systems do, and both will be obsolete the next time the capability
jumps.

I will keep saying AI, mostly because everyone I work with says AI, and the
entire value of a term of art is that it is shared. But I will keep trying to be
specific about the level, because that is the part that actually carries
information.
