When I said no to unlocking a set of drives by hand one more time (the story is
in [Under-Promise, Over-Deliver](/blog/under-promise-over-deliver/)), part of
the reason was about promises and expectations. The other part was simpler.
Unlocking drives by hand is not software engineering. Making it so nobody has to
unlock them by hand is.

I have written before that
[nobody gets promoted for shoveling](/blog/good-at-it-is-not-the-same-as-wanting-it/),
no matter how fast they shovel. That post was about toil and about what I want
to spend my time on. This one is about something more mechanical: what your work
gets measured against.

## The title is the yardstick

If you are a software engineer, and all you are doing is manual tooling work,
customer support, or anything else that is only loosely related to the main job,
you are not going to be promoted as a software engineer. It does not matter how
well you are doing that work. Promotion to the next software engineering level
is judged against software engineering (designing, building, and changing
systems) and against what the company needs from that role. Work outside the
role does not show up on that scale, because the scale was never built to read
it.

That is not a statement about the value of the work. Customer support is real
work, and somebody has to do the manual steps until they are automated. Those
jobs exist, and they have their own ladders. The problem is doing one job while
holding the title of another. You end up excellent at work that nobody is
evaluating you for, and short on the evidence the evaluation actually looks for.

The roles themselves are drawn around different kinds of work. In
[Overlapping Roles in Tech](/blog/overlapping-roles-in-tech/) I laid out which
roles share which work and why the overlaps need a named owner. Grunt work that
belongs to another role sits outside your circle entirely. And the organization
side of this is
[promotion-driven development](/blog/promotion-driven-development/): people
build toward the evidence the promotion system can read. Grunt work produces
very little of that evidence for an engineer, so a team that hands it to one
engineer is quietly holding that engineer back.

## "That is not my role" is allowed

It is okay to say no. It is okay to say "that is not my role," especially for
work that is not aligned to the role you were hired into and are evaluated
against. Saying it is not refusing to help. It is naming who should own the
work, so that it lands on the role that is measured for it, or on the
engineering fix that removes it.

In practice the no is rarely a bare no. For the drives it sounded like "not by
hand again, but give me two days and one more person and it goes away." That is
the engineering version of the request, and it is squarely inside the role.
Saying no to the manual version left room to say yes to the part that counts. I
made the team-level version of this argument in
[Minimize Humans as Glue](/blog/minimize-humans-as-glue/), where "start saying
no" was the first move out of being the person who holds everything together.
The difference here is whose work it is. Glue work is often your team's work
that nobody gets credit for. Grunt work outside your role is often another
role's work that ended up on your desk.

## Except at a startup

None of this applies the same way at a startup. A startup is a place where you
are going to wear many hats, and you should expect to. When there are eight
people, the software engineer also answers the customer, runs the deploy by
hand, and fixes the printer. That is not a misassigned role; that is the role.
The company needs all of it, and the company is small enough to see who did it.

The test I use is not "is this beneath me?" It is "does the role I am measured
against include this work?" At a startup the answer is often yes. At a larger
company, with a defined ladder and other teams whose job this is, the answer is
often no, and then the time I spend on it is time lent, not time invested. I can
lend it when it is needed. I should not let it become the job.
