I added up one day's calendar: five 30-minute meetings and one 45-minute
meeting. Three hours and fifteen minutes of scheduled talking, before counting
the ten minutes on either side of each block where I am not really working
either. The composition is what stopped me. Three were status meetings, two were
1:1s, and one was a requirements discussion.

The requirements meeting had to happen live. Someone had a question nobody had
written down yet, and the answer only existed in the conversation between three
people. The 1:1s had to happen live too, for a different reason: they are where
trust and context get built, and neither survives being converted into a
document. That is roughly one and a half hours of meeting time doing work that
only meetings can do.

Which leaves the three status meetings, and status is the one thing on that list
that a meeting is genuinely poor at.

## Status is a read operation

A status meeting synchronizes everyone's clock so one person can learn
something. Ten people block the same 30 minutes, each speaks for two of them,
and each listens to twenty-eight minutes of information they will not act on.
The cost is not the half hour on the calendar; it is that the half hour lands in
the middle of the afternoon and splits the day into two pieces too small to hold
a hard problem.

The information itself is not the problem. Someone needs to know where the work
stands, what is blocked, and what changed. That is a read of state. Reads do not
require everyone to be in the same room at the same time; they require the state
to be written down somewhere readable. When my work is already logged as I go,
the status meeting is a slower, lossier, more expensive version of a query I
could have answered in a link. I already keep that record for other reasons: a
[worklog](/blog/why-i-love-worklogs/) exists so context survives between
sessions, and it turns out the same artifact answers "what is the status?"
without anyone scheduling anything.

This is why async matters, and not for the reason it usually gets argued. Async
is not about preferring writing to talking or introverts to extroverts. Async is
what makes declining a meeting safe. If the only place the information exists is
in the meeting, then skipping the meeting means the information does not travel,
and the person who skipped is correctly seen as the one who broke the chain. If
the information exists in a durable, searchable place, then the meeting is
optional by construction, and declining it costs the team nothing. You cannot
opt out of a synchronous status ritual until you have replaced what it produced.
Build the replacement first, then decline.

## Declining is capacity management, not attitude

Individual contributors are, in my experience, unusually reluctant to decline a
recurring meeting. Part of it is that the recurring invite arrives once and then
never asks again; agreeing in March quietly commits every Tuesday through
December. Part of it is that declining feels like a statement about the people
in the room rather than about the arithmetic of the day.

It is arithmetic. A day has a fixed number of hours where I can hold a complex
problem in my head, and that number is well under eight. Three hours of meetings
does not remove three hours from that budget; it removes more, because the gaps
between them are too short to reload context. Treating my calendar as capacity
rather than as availability changes what a decline means. I am not saying the
meeting is worthless. I am saying my attention is a
[finite resource](/blog/burnout-is-a-control-problem/) being spent, and I am the
only one with a full view of what else it is committed to.

The useful move is not a blanket no. It is deciding which meetings only work
live and defending those, then converting the rest. In that day, the
requirements meeting and the two 1:1s earned their place; the three status
meetings did not. Naming the category out loud is what makes the conversation
tractable, because "I want fewer meetings" invites a debate about work ethic
while "status can be read instead of attended" invites a debate about tooling.
The second debate is one you can win.

There is a version of this that fails, and it fails the way glue work fails. If
I quietly decline the status meeting and then reconstruct the same information
by DMing four people individually, I have not removed the meeting, I have
converted a scheduled cost into an
[invisible one](/blog/minimize-humans-as-glue/) and made myself the router. The
decline only pays off if the state is genuinely written down where anyone can
read it, including the person who called the meeting in the first place.

## Who has to agree

The part I cannot do alone is the recurring invite itself. An individual
contributor can decline a single instance without much friction, but removing a
recurring status meeting from a team's calendar is a decision the meeting owner
has to make. What an IC can do is make the case with evidence: here is the
written record, here is how current it is, here is the last month of the meeting
where nothing was raised that was not already in the log.

That framing also protects the meetings worth keeping. When status stops
consuming the calendar, the 1:1s stop competing with it, and the requirements
conversation gets to run long when the question turns out to be harder than
anyone expected. Cutting meetings is not the goal. Spending the synchronous
hours on the work that actually needs a room is the goal.

_The day that started this was not unusual, which is the part worth sitting
with. Three hours and fifteen minutes was normal, and more than half of it was
performing a read out loud. I did not learn that I dislike meetings. I learned
that my calendar had been accepting recurring commitments on my behalf, and that
the way to get the time back was not to be more disciplined about attending, but
to make the information survive without me._
